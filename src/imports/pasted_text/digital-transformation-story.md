Inside a Digital Transformation

The story behind leading and strategizing 12+ redesigns inside an evolving enterprise ecosystem


---

SECTION 01

The redesign was visible. The transformation was still unfolding.

When I joined Infineon, the company was already investing heavily in digital transformation.

The redesign of the main corporate website was underway. External agencies had already been onboarded. UX teams were beginning to form internally. Conversations around digital experience had started becoming more visible across the organization.

From the outside, it looked like the transformation had already begun.

But internally, the ecosystem still operated in fragments.

Customer-facing experiences behaved differently from operational tools. Internal systems had evolved independently over years. Enterprise workflows lacked consistency. Interaction patterns varied drastically across products. Teams approached UX differently depending on the initiative.

The redesign initiative existed.

But the design culture behind it was still evolving in real time.

At that point, I was not leading the website redesign itself.

I joined after parts of the redesign had already been defined and development was already progressing. Another designer in the team was leading the core website experience while I initially joined as a supporting designer inside the broader transformation initiative.

But that early phase became incredibly important for my growth.

Because instead of immediately taking ownership of a single product, I spent the first few months understanding how the organization itself operated around UX.

I wanted to understand:

how decisions were made

how workflows moved between business and engineering

where usability friction repeatedly appeared

how disconnected systems influenced each other

where UX maturity gaps existed across the ecosystem


Very quickly, one thing became obvious.

The website was evolving.

But the surrounding ecosystem still behaved like disconnected products.

And the first project that exposed this clearly was the Infineon Community platform.

SECTION 02

The first redesign exposed a much larger ecosystem problem

At first glance, the Community redesign looked relatively straightforward.

The platform needed modernization. The experience felt visually outdated. Navigation lacked structure. Interactions no longer aligned with the direction the main website was moving toward.

Initially, it looked like a redesign project.

But once I started studying how users actually moved through the ecosystem, the scale of the problem became much larger than the interface itself.

The Community platform sat at the center of multiple user journeys simultaneously:

developer onboarding

technical troubleshooting

support discovery

product exploration

documentation access

knowledge sharing

ecosystem engagement


Which meant even relatively small usability gaps created friction across multiple connected workflows.

One of the earliest things I noticed was how disconnected the experience felt from the rest of the ecosystem.

A user could move from the redesigned website into Community and suddenly encounter:

different navigation logic

inconsistent interaction behaviors

duplicated information structures

varying usability standards

disconnected support pathways


The transition felt abrupt.

Not because individual screens were poorly designed.

But because connected systems had evolved without shared UX thinking.

That distinction completely changed how I approached the work.

The challenge was no longer: “How do we modernize this platform visually?”

It became: “How do we create continuity across connected experiences?”

So instead of approaching the redesign as a UI exercise, I started treating it as an ecosystem continuity problem.

I began mapping:

where users entered the ecosystem

how they moved between platforms

where support journeys broke down

how documentation and Community overlapped

where users repeatedly abandoned workflows

how engagement patterns differed across journeys


This phase involved:

usability studies

information architecture restructuring

flow redesign

stakeholder workshops

iterative testing

cross-functional alignment discussions with global teams

And this was also where I started seeing the organizational side of UX maturity very clearly.

Requirements often arrived partially defined. Stakeholders interpreted workflows differently. Priorities shifted constantly. And design discussions frequently happened after major decisions had already been made.

Which meant a significant part of the work became: creating alignment before designing interfaces.

Sometimes that meant facilitating conversations between engineering and business teams who visualized the same workflow completely differently.

Sometimes it meant reframing usability issues as structural workflow problems instead of cosmetic UI concerns.

And sometimes it simply meant slowing projects down enough for teams to rethink assumptions that had existed for years.

That was one of the first moments where my role started evolving beyond execution.

And once the Community redesign started gaining visibility internally, something important changed.

Other teams started approaching UX differently.

And more projects started arriving.

SECTION 03

The ecosystem started scaling faster than its UX maturity

After the Community redesign, the nature of the work changed dramatically.

What started as one redesign initiative gradually evolved into multiple transformation projects happening in parallel across the ecosystem.

Some projects focused on customer-facing workflows.

Others supported internal operational teams.

Some targeted distributors and sales enablement.

Others introduced AI-assisted experiences into enterprise environments that previously depended almost entirely on manual workflows.

And unlike the earlier website redesign, many of these products had no established UX direction at all.

SECTION 04

Somewhere along the process, the role became larger than the projects

As more initiatives started overlapping across the ecosystem, my role naturally became more collaborative and organizationally visible.

What initially began as redesign ownership gradually evolved into cross-functional coordination across globally distributed teams.

And one important part of this journey was navigating all of it as the only designer based in India within a largely global setup.

Most of the team operated from Munich and other international locations, while I collaborated remotely across products, stakeholders, engineering teams, researchers, and leadership conversations spread across regions and time zones.

Over time, this introduced a unique dynamic into the work.

A significant part of the role involved not only designing systems, but also continuously building alignment across:

product teams

engineering

researchers

business stakeholders

platform owners

design system collaborators

operational teams


And every year, I traveled to Munich to work more closely with the global team, align on future initiatives, participate in planning discussions, and help shape the direction of the upcoming phase of projects across the ecosystem.

Those visits became important turning points.

Because the conversations gradually shifted from: individual redesign execution

to: ecosystem direction, governance, scalability, and long-term UX strategy.

SECTION 05

AI introduced an entirely new layer of UX complexity

The next major transformation happened when AI-assisted products started entering the ecosystem.

And unlike earlier redesign initiatives, these projects introduced a completely different category of UX challenges.

One of the earliest examples was the Infineon Digital Assistant.

At the surface level, it looked like a conversational AI integrated into the website.

But the actual UX challenge was much larger than the interface itself.

Users were already navigating:

documentation systems

support workflows

distributor platforms

operational tools

technical product ecosystems


And suddenly AI was becoming another layer inside those journeys.

At that point:

no internal AI interaction standards existed

conversational UX patterns were inconsistent

governance for AI-assisted experiences did not yet exist

multiple teams were beginning AI initiatives independently


Which meant the ecosystem risked becoming fragmented all over again — this time through AI interactions.

systems thinking

ecosystem-level UX direction

And by this stage, something else had also changed.

Teams that initially involved UX only after requirements were finalized had started bringing me into conversations much earlier.

Sometimes before workflows existed.

Sometimes before product structures had been defined.

Sometimes before stakeholders themselves fully understood what the product needed to become.

And honestly, that shift represented one of the clearest signs that the organization’s UX maturity itself had evolved.

EPILOGUE

The transformation was never really about the interfaces

Looking back, the most meaningful part of this journey was never the number of redesigns themselves.

It was watching UX gradually evolve from isolated execution into a more embedded strategic capability across the ecosystem.

Over three years, the work expanded from: redesigning interfaces

into:

influencing systems

shaping workflows

aligning teams

scaling governance

standardizing enterprise interactions

introducing reusable AI patterns

connecting fragmented products

helping UX become part of larger strategic conversations


And importantly, none of that happened through one massive initiative.

It happened gradually.

Through repeated redesigns. Repeated workshops. Repeated alignment discussions. Repeated ecosystem problems. Repeated collaboration across globally distributed teams. Repeated attempts to prevent fragmentation as the ecosystem evolved.

The transformation itself is still ongoing.

But somewhere during the process, the work stopped becoming about designing products individually.

And started becoming about shaping how digital experiences evolve together inside complex systems.
